# Creation Guide

How to build a case study from raw project data. Read this and html-template.md
before generating any case study. Output is always a single-file HTML document saved
to the project's `drafts/` folder, never a markdown file.

Calibrate to Lead/Staff hiring bars, fall back to Senior framing only when the
material genuinely does not support more. Build the story around insights and
outcomes, not a chronological project diary.

## 4-panel teaser

The above-the-fold block a reviewer sees before scrolling. It must let someone decide
in ten seconds that the case study is worth reading. Four panels:

1. **Problem.** One line on the real problem and who had it. Not the feature name,
   the pain. "Guests could not access shared accounts without creating their own."
2. **Role.** Title, team, timeframe, and what the candidate owned versus shared.
   Honest scope, no inflation.
3. **Outcome.** The headline result, before to after, with a timeframe. This is the
   hook. If there is no quantified outcome, lead with the strongest qualitative one
   and say so plainly rather than implying a metric.
4. **Visual.** One strong image that shows the transformation, not a logo or a mood
   shot. The single most legible before/after or final surface.

Two metric placements maximum across the whole page: once in the teaser, once in the
outcomes band. Do not sprinkle the same number throughout.

## Themed sections

Organize the body by two to four themes, each a decision or insight that carried the
project, not by phase. A theme reads as a claim with evidence: a header stating what
happened (Action + Outcome), a short narrative, one supporting visual, and the
tradeoff or constraint that made it hard. Themes beat "Research / Ideation / Final
Design" because they show judgment, which is what levels a candidate up.

## Supporting sections

Around the themes, include only what earns its place:

- **Context** — product, users, business stakes, constraints. Tight.
- **Role and team** — who did what. Sets attribution once so the body can use "I"
  honestly.
- **Process** — shown through the themes, not as a separate method checklist. Surface
  a method only when its insight changed the design.
- **Reflection** — what the candidate learned or would do differently. Short, honest,
  senior. Avoid humblebrags and avoid trashing the work.

## Visual rules

- Fewer, larger screenshots. One before/after per theme at most.
- Captions tell the transformation, not the UI: "Before: settings scattered across
  five screens. After: one consolidated page." Not "Settings screen."
- GIFs for multi-step flows, static images for comparisons.
- One bold phrase per paragraph maximum; bold the phrase a scanner needs.
- Never show low-fidelity work unless it makes a point about the process.

## Fact-check checklist

Run before finalizing. Every item must pass or the content comes out.

- Every metric has a source file and a timeframe, and matches the source snapshot.
- Every quote is verbatim or near-verbatim from a source and attributable to a real
  person about this project. No generated quotes attributed to named people.
- Every "I" is solo work; shared work uses co-facilitated / partnered / coordinated.
- No scope overclaim: the visuals and system maps match what the candidate actually
  designed, not the surrounding ecosystem.
- Vision or concept work is labelled as such, never implied as shipped.
- Anything that cannot be traced to a source is flagged to the user, not placed.

## Tone guide

- Conversational and confident. Story, not resume bullet.
- No em dashes. Use commas, full stops, or restructure the sentence.
- No corporate jargon (leveraged, spearheaded, holistic, synergy).
- No AI-sounding tells (what sets X apart, it is worth noting, I would be remiss).
- No self-declared skill labels (UX Strategy, Systems Thinking). Let the work show it.
- Concise. Cut filler paragraphs. Match the user's language.

Before / after voice:
- Weak: "I leveraged a holistic research approach to spearhead a best-in-class flow."
- Strong: "I ran five diary studies, found people gave up at the upload step, and cut
  the flow from seven screens to three."

## Page structure template

1. Nav / candidate name
2. Title + one-line subtitle
3. 4-panel teaser (problem, role, outcome, visual)
4. Context (short)
5. Theme 1 (header, narrative, visual, tradeoff)
6. Theme 2
7. Theme 3 (optional)
8. Outcomes band (the metric, sourced, second and final placement)
9. Reflection (short)
10. Footer (contact, next project)

## Anonymisation rules

For NDA or confidential work:

- Replace the company with a descriptor ("a Fortune 500 utility," "a large ride-share
  platform") consistently throughout.
- Convert absolute metrics to relative where required ("increased completion 22
  percent" rather than raw counts), and say the numbers are directional.
- Blur or redact proprietary UI, real names, and unreleased features in screenshots;
  redraw as generic wireframes if needed.
- Never anonymise by fabricating a different but "equivalent" story. Anonymise the
  identifying details, keep the facts true.
- State once, quietly, that details are anonymised under NDA. Do not over-explain.

## Project type adaptations

- **0 to 1 / new product** — lead with problem framing and the bet. Outcomes may be
  early (adoption, qualitative signal); label them as early and honest.
- **Redesign** — before/after is the spine. Show the old experience's specific
  failures, then the measured improvement.
- **Design system** — impact is leverage: adoption across teams, consistency, speed.
  Show the system others reused, not one screen.
- **Research-heavy** — the insight and the decision it changed are the product. Guard
  hard against method-checklist framing.
- **Growth / experimentation** — center the hypothesis, the experiment, and the
  metric movement, with honest caveats on measurement.
- **Platform / tools / internal** — quantify saved time or reduced errors for the
  internal users; make the invisible work legible.
